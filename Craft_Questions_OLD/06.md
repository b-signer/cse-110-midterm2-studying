## The AI Revolution and Process Models

**Q: If AI is a big change like the dotCom boom that spurred Agile, what process model would you imagine being adopted?**

Just as the dotCom era's demand for rapid, iterative delivery made Waterfall's rigid phase-gates obsolete and gave rise to Agile, an AI-driven revolution would likely produce a process model centered on continuous, human-in-the-loop validation rather than human-authored iteration. We might see something resembling a "Generative-Iterative" model: AI agents draft code, tests, and even requirements, while human engineers shift primarily to review, prompt engineering, and architectural oversight. The model would compress the design-to-deployment cycle dramatically, but would place outsized emphasis on evaluation frameworks, output auditing, and rapid feedback — because the bottleneck is no longer writing code but verifying that AI-generated code is correct, secure, and aligned with intent. Governance, traceability, and ethical guardrails would be first-class process artifacts rather than afterthoughts, analogous to how Agile elevated user stories and retrospectives into core ceremonies.

---

**Q: Would the activities change in the SDLC, or would we just change the emphasis of steps?**

The fundamental activities of the SDLC — requirements, design, implementation, testing, deployment, and maintenance — are unlikely to disappear, because they reflect the logical dependencies of delivering working software. What AI fundamentally changes is the *distribution of effort* across those phases. Implementation, historically the most labor-intensive phase, becomes largely automated, so the emphasis shifts heavily toward requirements elicitation (garbage-in-garbage-out applies acutely to AI generation) and testing/validation (since AI output can be subtly wrong in ways that are hard to detect). Maintenance and prompt refinement may effectively merge into a new continuous phase with no clear boundary. In short, the activities remain, but the human role within each activity transforms from authoring to directing and verifying — a shift in emphasis far more than a structural overhaul of the SDLC itself.

## The People Counter Argument

**Q: If we could apply Taylorist "Scientific Software Engineering" and swap out one developer for another, what would be required in terms of formality and definition? Does this actually make sense given the nature of SWE work?**

For Taylorist interchangeability to work in software engineering, you would need exhaustively formal process definitions: every task decomposed into precise, documented subtasks with explicit inputs, outputs, acceptance criteria, coding standards, architectural diagrams, and decision rationale — essentially a specification so complete that any competent engineer could pick it up mid-stream without context loss. In civil engineering this approximates reality because laying rebar follows a physical specification. In SWE, however, the "material" is logic and abstraction, and enormous amounts of critical knowledge live in developers' heads: why a particular architectural tradeoff was made, what edge cases a module handles, the implicit assumptions baked into an API contract. Consider a scenario where a developer three months into building a payments service is suddenly replaced: the incoming engineer reads the spec, but the original dev knew that a specific bank's API silently truncates amounts over a certain precision — knowledge earned through debugging that never made it into any document. No amount of formalism fully captures that tacit, contextual expertise, which is why pure Taylorist substitution in SWE consistently fails and why practices like pair programming, documentation culture, and knowledge-sharing are treated as risk mitigation rather than optional niceties.

## Risk, Time, and Money

**Q: Much of process modeling is about lowering risk and improving predictability of on-time, on-budget delivery. Using the analogy of building a house, discuss what we do to lower risk and predictability, and provide vivid examples of things still going wrong.**

Building a house employs numerous risk-reduction strategies that map directly onto software: architects produce blueprints (requirements/design artifacts) to catch structural conflicts before any concrete is poured; permitting processes enforce regulatory checkpoints; contractors provide fixed-price bids scoped against detailed specs; and phased inspections (foundation, framing, electrical) catch defects before they are buried under subsequent work. Despite all of this, projects routinely go wrong. A homeowner signs a contract for a $400,000 remodel on a six-month timeline; the crew breaks ground and discovers the foundation has undiagnosed subsidence requiring $60,000 in remediation — a risk no blueprint can surface until the earth is opened. The contractor, now behind schedule, rushes the framing to recover time, and the city inspector fails it for improper load-bearing wall placement. Materials ordered months in advance are backordered due to supply chain disruption, adding another six weeks. By completion the project is $110,000 over budget and four months late. Every bet placed at contract signing — that the foundation was sound, that the supply chain was stable, that the crew could recover lost time — turned out to be wrong. The parallel in software is striking: initial estimates assume known scope and stable requirements, yet discovered complexity (the software equivalent of a bad foundation) and changing external dependencies routinely invalidate every original commitment, which is precisely why process models exist to make those bets more informed rather than to eliminate them.

## Which Process Model Makes Sense?

**Q: Given a description of a domain, organization, team, or type of software, how do you suggest the appropriate level of formality and process? Walk through your reasoning.**

Choosing a process model is fundamentally about matching the cost of change to the rate of change in requirements, and calibrating overhead to the consequences of failure. In highly regulated domains — avionics (DO-178C), medical devices (FDA 21 CFR Part 11), or nuclear control systems — a formal, plan-driven model such as Waterfall or the V-Model is appropriate because regulators mandate documented traceability from requirement to test, and a defect can cost lives; the overhead of extensive upfront documentation is justified by the catastrophic cost of a downstream defect. Conversely, an early-stage startup building a consumer mobile app operates under near-total requirements uncertainty and must learn from user feedback rapidly; a heavyweight planning and documentation regime would burn runway on artifacts that will be obsolete by the next pivot, making a lean Agile approach (Scrum or Kanban) far more appropriate. A mid-sized enterprise building internal tooling for a stable business process sits in between: requirements are relatively known but not life-critical, so a hybrid approach — iterative sprints within a loosely defined roadmap — balances adaptability with enough structure for stakeholder communication. The heuristic is: the higher the cost of a defect and the more stable the requirements, the more formality is warranted; the higher the uncertainty and the lower the failure cost, the lighter the process should be.

## The Cone of Uncertainty

**Q: Explain what the Cone of Uncertainty represents, then argue why the cone always narrowing may not reflect project reality.**

The Cone of Uncertainty illustrates that early in a project, estimates of schedule, cost, and scope carry enormous variance — the "cone" is wide — because so little is known: the technology is unproven, requirements are vague, and team velocity is unmeasured. As the project progresses and unknowns are resolved through actual work, the cone narrows, reflecting tighter, more accurate estimates. The intuitive appeal is that learning reduces uncertainty monotonically. In practice, however, the cone can widen mid-project rather than consistently narrow. Consider a team eight months into developing a mobile banking app: they have resolved the authentication architecture and established velocity, so uncertainty on those fronts is low. But the product manager returns from a stakeholder meeting announcing that regulatory guidance has shifted and biometric authentication must now meet a new federal standard not yet finalized — a requirement that didn't exist at project start. Suddenly the scope and timeline estimates blow out again; the cone widens. Similarly, a third-party payment processor the team depended on announces deprecation of the API being integrated, forcing a mid-flight architectural change. Real software projects exist in dynamic environments where external forces — regulatory shifts, vendor changes, market pivots, team turnover — can re-introduce uncertainty at any phase, meaning the cone is better understood as a general tendency than a guaranteed monotonic narrowing.

## Applied Iron Triangle

**Q: Given an Iron Triangle diagram, explain how it relates to a team project. Be prepared to calculate developer availability in person-hours and relate it to feature scope, and explain why estimation is critical but imprecise.**

The Iron Triangle constrains every software project across three vertices: Scope (features/quality), Time (schedule), and Cost (budget/resources) — and the fundamental rule is that fixing any two vertices forces the third to flex. On a team project with, say, four developers working 10 hours per week over an 8-week quarter, total developer availability is 4 × 10 × 8 = 320 person-hours. If each feature is estimated at 20 person-hours (including design, implementation, and testing), the team can theoretically deliver 16 features — but that assumes 100% efficiency, no meetings, no debugging surprises, and perfect estimates, all of which are unrealistic. In practice, applying a realistic utilization factor (often 60–70%) yields closer to 190–224 effective person-hours, or roughly 9–11 features. This is why estimation is critical: underestimating feature complexity means the team commits to 16 features, delivers 9, and either ships late (Time flexes), cuts scope (Scope flexes), or adds developers — which, per Brooks's Law, often slows the project further by increasing coordination overhead (Cost flexes without improving Time). The Iron Triangle doesn't tell you which vertex to sacrifice; it tells you that you must choose consciously and communicate that choice to stakeholders rather than silently absorbing overruns.

## Thinking in Bets

**Q: Frame a concrete software project decision using the "Thinking in Bets" model — identify cost, reward, and % risk, and provide one good bet and one bad bet.**

Thinking in Bets, drawn from Annie Duke's framework, reframes decisions not as right/wrong choices but as expected-value wagers made under uncertainty: a good bet has a positive expected value even if it sometimes loses, and a bad bet has a negative expected value even if it occasionally wins. **Good bet:** A team is three weeks from launch and discovers their home-grown authentication module has a potential session fixation vulnerability. Patching it in-house will take an estimated 40 engineer-hours (cost), delay launch by one week (cost), and eliminate an estimated 80% probability of a breach that would trigger regulatory fines and user attrition worth roughly $200,000 (reward). Expected value of patching: high — the probability-weighted cost of a breach ($160,000) dwarfs the patch cost (~$6,000 in labor plus one week of delayed revenue). This is a good bet. **Bad bet:** That same team, eager to recover the lost week, decides to skip integration testing on the payment flow to ship on the original date — saving ~20 engineer-hours but accepting a ~40% probability of a critical billing bug reaching production, where fixing it post-launch involves emergency on-call response, customer refunds, and reputation damage estimated at $80,000. Expected cost of skipping: 0.4 × $80,000 = $32,000, far exceeding the $3,000 in saved labor. This is a bad bet — one that feels rational under schedule pressure but fails straightforwardly on expected value. The framework's power is that it forces engineers to make implicit risk assumptions explicit and numerical rather than hiding them behind optimism.
