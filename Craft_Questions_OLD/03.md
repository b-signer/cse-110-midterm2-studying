## Visualizing the Team Life Steps

**Q: Teams go through a common set of steps. Define each step and describe specifically what would happen at each one, including what YOU would want to happen.**

The most widely cited model of team development is Tuckman's stages: **Forming, Storming, Norming, Performing**, and **Adjourning**.

**Forming:** The team comes together for the first time; individuals are polite, uncertain of their roles, and dependent on the team leader for direction. What should happen: introductions that go beyond names and titles to expose skills, interests, and working styles; clear articulation of the team's purpose and initial structure; and early social investment to establish rapport before work pressure mounts. An ideal kickoff includes both technical orientation and a short team agreement exercise on communication norms.

**Storming:** Conflict emerges as personalities clash, roles are contested, and the gap between expectation and reality becomes visible. What should happen: the team lead creates space for disagreements to surface productively rather than fester; issues of workload distribution, decision-making authority, and technical approach are negotiated explicitly. Left unaddressed, storming-phase conflict goes underground and destroys productivity silently.

**Norming:** The team establishes working agreements, roles clarify, and trust begins to build. What should happen: formalize the norms that emerged organically — document the team's agreed process for code review, meeting cadence, and escalation paths. This is the phase where good habits get institutionalized, or bad ones do.

**Performing:** The team operates with high cohesion, low friction, and strong output. Members cover for each other, communication is efficient, and there is enough trust to have direct technical debates without relationship cost. This is the goal state.

**Adjourning:** The team disbands or reconfigures after project completion. What should happen: explicit retrospective to capture lessons learned, acknowledgment of members' contributions, and deliberate knowledge transfer so the team's accumulated understanding does not evaporate.

## Joining a Team

**Q: Describe 2–3 things a team and 2–3 things the joining member can do to make onboarding a success.**

**What the team should do:**
1. **Assign a dedicated onboarding buddy** — a single point of contact the new member can ask questions without filtering, reducing the social friction of interrupting people mid-work.
2. **Provide a working, documented local dev environment setup** — nothing signals team maturity and respect for the newcomer's time like a README that actually works. Broken setup steps on day one set a toxic tone and waste hours.
3. **Schedule intentional context transfer sessions** — brief walkthroughs of the codebase architecture, product domain, and team conventions, rather than expecting the new member to absorb this via osmosis from reading old tickets.

**What the joining member should do:**
1. **Bias toward asking rather than assuming** — the cost of a "dumb" question is seconds; the cost of building on a wrong assumption for two weeks is enormous.
2. **Observe before proposing changes** — earn trust by demonstrating understanding of why things are the way they are before suggesting they should be different. Teams are rightly skeptical of newcomers who arrive proposing to rewrite everything.
3. **Contribute something small and visible early** — fixing a bug, improving a test, or clarifying documentation proves good faith, builds relationships, and builds your own mental model of the codebase simultaneously.

## Leaving a Team

**Q: Describe in a bullet list what you should do when exiting a team, and what the team should do as well.**

**The departing member should:**
- Document all active work in progress — context, current state, blockers, and next steps — so the handoff is not just a verbal summary that evaporates.
- Identify and document any areas where they hold unique knowledge ("only I know why X works this way") and take deliberate steps to transfer it.
- Complete or formally hand off open pull requests and in-flight tickets to named owners.
- Conduct 1:1 knowledge transfer sessions with whoever will absorb their responsibilities.
- Communicate departure timelines clearly and early, giving the team enough runway to plan.
- Exit positively — burn no bridges; the industry is smaller than it appears.

**The team should:**
- Treat the departure as a bus factor event and use it to assess and reduce other single points of failure proactively.
- Update onboarding documentation based on gaps revealed by the departure.
- Redistribute work explicitly rather than letting it fall implicitly to whoever is most available.
- Conduct a lightweight retrospective on what the departure reveals about knowledge distribution and team resilience.
- Acknowledge the departing member's contributions formally — exits with recognition signal to remaining members that their work is valued.

## How Do Teams Survive Members Leaving?

**Q: What approaches should teams take to survive the loss of single members, multiple members, and full team? Be specific about artifacts, activities, and practices.**

**Single member loss:** The primary mitigation is continuous knowledge sharing through practices like pair programming, mob programming sessions, and thorough code review — ensuring that every significant piece of code is understood by at least two team members. Architecture Decision Records (ADRs), runbooks, and well-maintained wikis capture the institutional knowledge that lives in individuals' heads. Cross-training exercises (rotating ownership of subsystems) build redundancy before it is urgently needed.

**Multiple member loss:** This requires more structural resilience. Teams should maintain comprehensive onboarding documentation sufficient for a new member to become productive without relying on any single current member's guidance. CI/CD pipelines, automated test suites, and infrastructure-as-code reduce operational dependency on specific individuals' tribal knowledge. A well-documented system architecture makes it possible for new contributors to understand system boundaries without archaeology.

**Full team loss:** This is the scenario that demands the highest documentation discipline. The surviving artifacts — source code, test suite, deployment configs, architecture docs, and decision records — must be sufficient for a completely fresh team to understand and continue the project. Open-source projects that survive complete contributor turnover do so because their documentation and contribution guides are written for complete strangers. The useful audit question is: "Could a new team, given only our artifacts, continue this work?" The closer a team is to answering yes, the more resilient it is.

## Characteristics of Good & Bad Teammates

**Q: Describe the characteristics of good teammates and the tendencies and actions of bad teammates.**

**Good teammates:** Communicate proactively and honestly — flagging blockers early rather than silently grinding on a dead end. They do what they commit to doing, and when they cannot, they say so early. They give specific, constructive code review feedback and receive feedback without defensiveness. They lift others up — sharing knowledge, mentoring, and celebrating team wins rather than hoarding credit. They are flexible about their role rather than rigidly defending territory. They show up to meetings prepared and on time, and they keep communication concise and relevant.

**Bad teammates:** Ghost on communication — slow to respond, missing from standups, unreachable when blockers arise. Overpromise and underdeliver without transparency about why. Give either no code review feedback (rubber-stamping everything) or purely negative feedback without constructive alternatives. Create knowledge silos by failing to document or share context. Attribute team successes to themselves and failures to others. Engage in HiPPO dynamics — deferring entirely to whoever has the highest status rather than contributing independent judgment. The subtler version of bad teamwork is passive withdrawal: technically present but not contributing, saying yes in meetings and doing nothing afterward, completing only easy visible tasks while leaving hard problems for others.

## Team as Output

**Q: Jason Gorman says the actual result we should focus on is more the team than the product. Explain this in your own words using an example.**

Gorman's provocation is that a software product has a finite lifespan — it will be deprecated, replaced, or abandoned — but a team that has learned to function well together is a durable, reusable capability. The product is an artifact of the team; the team is the underlying generator. If a team ships a mediocre product but emerges from the experience with strong communication norms, deep mutual trust, established code review practices, and a shared mental model of how to approach complex problems, that team is a highly valuable organizational asset that will produce better outcomes on its next project. Conversely, a team that ships a successful product through heroic individual effort, chronic crunch, and interpersonal dysfunction has destroyed itself in the process — the product exists, but the capability to repeat or improve it does not. Concrete example: Team A ships a payment feature on time through one person working nights and weekends, with no documentation and no knowledge transfer. Team B ships slightly late but with comprehensive tests, an architecture walkthrough, and three engineers who can each maintain any part of the system. Team A's product exists; Team A as a functioning unit does not. Team B's product and team both persist — and Team B's next feature will be faster and better, compounding the investment in the team itself.

## The Google Study

**Q: Name and explain the five factors from Google's Project Aristotle that make for high-functioning teams. Which is most important and why?**

Google's Project Aristotle identified five key factors for high-performing teams:

**1. Psychological Safety:** Team members feel safe to take interpersonal risks — to speak up, admit mistakes, ask questions, or propose unconventional ideas — without fear of punishment or humiliation. This is the foundation on which all other factors rest.

**2. Dependability:** Team members reliably complete quality work on time. When someone commits to something, it gets done — creating a trust baseline that reduces the overhead of verification and escalation.

**3. Structure and Clarity:** Roles, responsibilities, and goals are clearly defined and understood. Members know what is expected of them, how their work relates to the team's objectives, and how decisions are made.

**4. Meaning:** The work is personally meaningful to team members — whether through the product's impact, professional growth, team relationships, or financial reward.

**5. Impact:** Members believe their work matters — that it contributes to something beyond themselves, whether the organization, users, or society.

Psychological safety is the most important factor because it is the meta-condition that enables all others. A team with high dependability but low psychological safety will surface problems only after they've compounded; a team with structural clarity but no safety will follow process even when the process is producing bad outcomes, rather than speaking up. Psychological safety is what allows a team to use its collective intelligence rather than just individual intelligence — it turns a group of people into an actual team. For the "your CSE SWE team" variant of this question, describe a concrete moment where psychological safety was or wasn't present and what the observable effect was.

## Bus Factor — Why and Fixes

**Q: Define bus factor. Provide an example in your SWE project context and discuss mitigations.**

The bus factor (sometimes called the "truck factor") of a project is the minimum number of team members who, if suddenly lost — hit by a proverbial bus, leaving the company, falling ill — would cause the project to fail or grind to a halt. A bus factor of 1 means a single person's departure is project-critical. In a typical CSE 110 team project, bus factor of 1 arises naturally: the one developer who set up the CI/CD pipeline, the one person who wrote the database schema and knows why certain columns are nullable, or the team lead who is the only one with deployment credentials. Mitigations operate at three levels: the **artifact level** (document everything that lives in one person's head — runbooks, architecture notes, credentials in a shared vault), the **practice level** (pair programming, rotating subsystem ownership, mandatory PR reviews so at least two people understand every significant change), and the **process level** (bus factor audits — periodically asking "if X disappeared tomorrow, what breaks?" and treating the answer as a risk to actively mitigate). For the exam, tie the example to a specific, named component of your actual project.

## Team Composition and Work Type

**Q: Discuss the tradeoffs between generalist team members versus specialists, and relate this to the type of work performed.**

Generalist teams, where every member can work across the full stack, offer maximum flexibility and resilience: anyone can pick up any task, there are no bottlenecks waiting for "the backend person" or "the database expert," and knowledge is distributed broadly. This model excels on early-stage projects where the product shape is uncertain and the team needs to iterate rapidly across the full system without organizational friction. The cost is depth: generalists rarely achieve the level of mastery in any given domain that produces the most efficient or highest-quality implementations. Specialist teams, conversely, excel at well-defined, stable domains where deep expertise produces disproportionate value — a dedicated security engineer finds vulnerabilities that generalists miss; a database specialist designs schemas and query plans that generalists wouldn't know to consider. The cost is coordination overhead and bottleneck risk: specialized work creates serial dependencies where teams wait on each other. In practice, the most effective engineering teams reflect the T-shaped model at the individual level — broad enough to contribute across boundaries, deep enough to bring genuine expertise to their domain. The optimal composition depends heavily on the product: systems with high domain complexity (security, ML, distributed systems) reward specialization; early-stage products requiring rapid, cross-cutting iteration reward generalism.

## Conway's Law

**Q: Conway's Law suggests that software architecture mirrors organizational structure. If the architecture is bad, what must we do outside of just focusing on the code?**

Conway's Law observes that "any organization that designs a system will produce a design whose structure is a copy of the organization's communication structure." If a company has three backend teams and two frontend teams with formal communication barriers between them, the software will tend to have three backend services and two frontend applications with an awkward integration boundary — because the architecture reflects the path of least organizational resistance. The implication for addressing poor architecture is significant: you cannot reliably fix the software architecture without restructuring the team topology, because the organizational structure will continuously regenerate the problematic architectural patterns even as engineers work to eliminate them. Practically, this means that an architectural transformation effort must be accompanied by team restructuring — if you want a modular monolith instead of a tangled web of services, you may need to combine teams or restructure ownership boundaries. The "Inverse Conway Maneuver" deliberately designs team structures to match the desired target architecture, betting that the code will follow organizational alignment. Team Topologies (the framework by Skelton and Pais) formalizes this into four team types (stream-aligned, enabling, complicated-subsystem, platform) structured to produce loosely coupled, independently deployable architectures — an organizational solution to what initially presents as an architectural problem.
