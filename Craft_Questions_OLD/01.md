## Defining Software Engineering in Your Own Words

**Q: As there appears to be no exacting definition of SE, please attempt to explain it yourself using common definitions and how you understand it. Try to include the ideas from the class, Wikipedia, etc. for a complete discussion.**

Software Engineering is the disciplined, systematic application of engineering principles to the design, development, testing, deployment, and maintenance of software systems — with the goal of producing reliable, efficient, and maintainable software on time and within budget. Wikipedia defines it as "a systematic engineering approach to software development," emphasizing that it goes beyond mere programming by incorporating methodology, rigor, and process. In practice, SE encompasses far more than writing code: it includes requirements engineering, architecture design, project management, quality assurance, documentation, and team coordination. What distinguishes SE from casual programming is the deliberate concern for scale, longevity, correctness, and the involvement of multiple stakeholders across a software product's entire lifecycle. Critically, SE is not purely a technical discipline — it is deeply social, organizational, and even economic in nature, because software is ultimately built by teams of people for other people, embedded within organizational and market contexts that shape every decision from tooling choices to deployment cadence.

## Why So Many Definitions?

**Q: Now that you have defined SE in your own words and acknowledged the varying definitions, explain why there are so many variations. Hint: Consider breadth of topics, type of projects, risk & impact, practitioner background, and more.**

The multiplicity of SE definitions reflects the field's unusual breadth and the diversity of contexts in which software is built. First, the domain is extraordinarily wide: SE applies equally to a safety-critical avionics control system requiring formal verification and a two-week hackathon prototype, yet the engineering discipline appropriate for each looks almost nothing alike. Second, practitioners come from wildly different backgrounds — mathematics, electrical engineering, self-teaching, computer science — each bringing a different conceptual lens that colors what they consider "engineering." Third, risk and impact vary enormously: a bug in a video game is embarrassing; a bug in pacemaker firmware kills people, and high-stakes domains tend to define SE through the lens of rigor, certification, and traceability, while consumer software domains emphasize speed and iteration. Fourth, the field is young compared to civil or mechanical engineering, and its "materials" — logic, abstraction, and information — have no physical analogues that impose natural constraints, making it harder to establish a single canonical definition. Finally, industry incentives, academic traditions, and cultural norms around what constitutes "good" software practice differ across companies and countries, further fragmenting consensus. All of these forces together produce a landscape where every community defines SE slightly differently to reflect its own priorities.

## To Professionalize SWE or Not?

**Q: Do you think it is useful for us to professionalize, to have certification, standards and practices, and so on? Explain why you think this is a good idea and a bad idea. The best answers may argue both sides to show deep consideration.**

The case for professionalizing software engineering is compelling when examined through the lens of public safety and accountability. In fields like civil or aeronautical engineering, licensure exists precisely because poor practice can kill people; increasingly, software failures carry the same stakes — faulty medical device software, autonomous vehicle control bugs, and insecure critical infrastructure have caused real harm. A licensed SE profession could establish minimum competency standards, create ethical obligations enforceable beyond a single employer's interests, and provide society with recourse when software systems cause harm. Professional certification could also elevate the field's credibility, improve knowledge standardization, and reduce the wide variance in practitioner quality that plagues the industry today. However, the case against professionalization is equally strong. Software engineering is so diverse in its domains, methodologies, and required knowledge that a single licensing framework risks enshrining one paradigm as correct — likely the most formal and bureaucratic one — at the expense of the iterative, experimental approaches that have produced some of the most valuable software in history. Certification processes tend to lag behind technology by years, making them ill-suited to a field that reinvents its toolchain every decade. They can also create gatekeeping barriers that exclude talented self-taught engineers while certifying mediocre ones who test well. The optimal position is probably domain-specific professionalization — mandatory licensure for software embedded in life-critical systems, voluntary certification elsewhere — rather than a blanket professional mandate.

## Externalities and SWE

**Q: Provide one example from the world or industry you have run across and discuss its effects on software and its creation for 1–3 paragraphs.**

The rise of large language models and AI code generation tools — most visibly GitHub Copilot, ChatGPT, and Claude — represents a profound external effect reshaping software engineering practice in ways that have little to do with the code itself. The economic externality is straightforward: investor capital poured into AI companies has created market pressure for every software organization to integrate AI tooling, regardless of whether doing so makes engineering sense for a given team or product. This creates a "cargo cult" dynamic where adoption is driven by competitive anxiety and investor signaling rather than measured engineering judgment — a phenomenon familiar to anyone who remembers the "we need blockchain" wave of 2017–2019.

The organizational effects run deeper. AI code generation raises fundamental questions about code ownership, intellectual property, and legal liability: when an AI trained on open-source code produces output that closely resembles a GPL-licensed project, who is responsible? Multiple ongoing lawsuits — GitHub Copilot copyright cases, The New York Times vs. OpenAI — are actively reshaping the legal landscape for software companies that depend on AI-assisted development, creating regulatory uncertainty that directly affects architecture decisions such as whether to use AI-generated code in products sold to regulated industries. Teams are also restructuring around AI capabilities, with some organizations reducing junior headcount under the assumption that AI handles entry-level tasks, which has downstream effects on mentorship pipelines and team knowledge transfer that are not yet well understood. The externality is not the tool itself but the economic, legal, and organizational forces it has set in motion.

## Software >>> Code

**Q: Enumerate 3–5 artifacts beyond the source code that are part of software. Besides listing them, explain why each is important and/or how it is used.**

**1. Requirements Specification:** A requirements document (user stories, use cases, or formal SRS) captures what the system must do from stakeholders' perspectives before a line of code is written. Without it, development proceeds on implicit assumptions that fragment across the team; with it, all parties have a shared, negotiable reference that drives design decisions and acceptance testing. It is also the primary artifact for managing scope creep.

**2. Architecture/Design Documentation:** Diagrams, ADRs (Architecture Decision Records), and design specs document the high-level structure of the system — component boundaries, data flows, technology choices, and the rationale behind them. This artifact is critical for onboarding new engineers, reasoning about the impact of changes, and avoiding the architectural drift that occurs when implementation diverges from original intent without anyone noticing.

**3. Test Suite:** Automated tests (unit, integration, end-to-end) are software artifacts in their own right. They serve as executable specifications that verify system behavior, catch regressions, and provide a safety net for refactoring. A codebase without a test suite is effectively undocumented behavior — the tests are what prove the code does what the team thinks it does.

**4. Deployment and Infrastructure Configuration:** CI/CD pipeline definitions, container specifications (Dockerfiles), and infrastructure-as-code (Terraform, Kubernetes manifests) encode how the software is built, tested, and deployed. As systems move to cloud-native architectures, these artifacts become as critical as the application code itself — a correct application deployed incorrectly is still a broken system.

**5. User and Operations Documentation:** README files, runbooks, API documentation, and incident response guides enable the humans who use, operate, and maintain the system to do so effectively. Without these, tribal knowledge accumulates in individuals and creates single points of failure, and the system becomes progressively unmaintainable as original authors leave.

## Things Learned and Views Changed (Optional — Industry or Industry Required)

**Q: Reflect on what you believed as an academically trained SWE and what you believe now after being exposed to industry. Use the attached blog post as a guide.**

One of the most common shifts from academic to industry SWE is the realization that code quality, while important, is never the primary constraint — organizational dynamics, stakeholder communication, and technical debt management frequently matter more to project outcomes than algorithmic elegance. In school, success is measured by correctness and cleverness; in industry, it is measured by delivery, maintainability, and team velocity. A perfectly designed system that ships six months late or that no one else on the team can understand is a failure by industry standards. Similarly, many engineers discover that "best practices" like TDD, SOLID principles, and microservices architectures are context-dependent tools rather than universal laws — applied dogmatically they can slow teams down and introduce unnecessary complexity. The deeper lesson is that engineering judgment, not adherence to any framework, is the actual skill that industry rewards. ## Could SWE be More a Social Science?

**Q: Since people are a huge aspect of software engineering, is the field more related to social sciences than hard sciences? Discuss comparisons, contrast with other engineering disciplines, and consider how you would replicate a SWE study.**

Software engineering occupies an unusual position: it has the formal rigor vocabulary of engineering (algorithms, complexity, correctness proofs) but the messy, context-dependent, people-driven reality of a social science. Consider that the primary determinants of software project success identified by decades of research — team communication quality, psychological safety, stakeholder alignment, organizational culture — are social phenomena, not technical ones. By this measure, SE resembles sociology or organizational psychology more than civil engineering, where material properties are deterministic and scale reliably. Civil engineers can calculate load-bearing capacity from first principles; software teams cannot calculate delivery dates from first principles because the "material" — human attention, creativity, and coordination — does not behave uniformly. Compared to aeronautical engineering, where every component's failure mode can be tested in isolation and combined using well-understood physics, SWE systems interact with human behavior at every layer, making emergent failures nearly impossible to predict from component-level analysis alone. The methodological challenge of replicating SWE studies underscores this: a study showing that practice X improves team velocity at Company A is extremely difficult to replicate at Company B because team composition, organizational culture, codebase history, and management style — all of which vary — confound every result. This is precisely the replication problem that social sciences grapple with, and it suggests that SWE needs mixed-methods research designs combining quantitative metrics with ethnographic observation, rather than expecting the clean controlled experiments that hard sciences rely on.
