## Common Advice

### UI Evaluation Principles

- No fancy pixels — keep it rough at first
- Recognition over recall (multi-choice test story)
- Less work, more gain
- Respect conventions
- Brand the buttons; know what's clickable; respect the L-shape real estate of a screen
- Embrace the "99% rule"
- Reject convention at appropriate times — death by sameness and regularity
- "What's Behind Door #1?" — embrace context of use (device, situation)
- **Rule: Play UI Golf** — minimize the number of steps to accomplish a goal
- **Evaluate:** Be quiet, observe others, don't guide them to your conclusions

---

### Marry Decisions Carefully

Architecture, dependencies, frameworks, and tools can be difficult to undo. Use ADRs and plan carefully — these are marriage-level decisions.

---

### Great Devs Steal (Thoughtfully)

You should see others doing things like what you want to do — that's a reasonable and good sign. You just want your own twist and your own attention to detail. No triangular pizzas; just better pizzas.

---

### It's Your Project

Consult users and stakeholders, but take a stand. If you can defend your decisions and followed the process, that's valid. If you don't love your product, users probably won't either — **dog food it** (use it yourself).

---

### A Rough Approach to Building

1. **Constraints are advantages** — they focus everything
2. **Code from the core** — start with the core interface/interaction, not the edges (except when inertia is an issue)
3. **Less features, more focus** — don't build everything users ask for; understand the underlying problem and what you need at the core
4. **Love your software** — build something you yourself need; you'll make better decisions
5. **Ship early, ship often** — a half-product that's real beats a perfect product that never ships
6. **Get feedback and iterate — mostly** — listen to user and stakeholder feedback and evolve the software, but be careful not to lose focus; learn to say no

---

### Jargon — The Good, Bad, and Ugly

Jargon can be both efficient and inclusive for those who understand it, and excluding and confusing for those who don't. Avoid too much jargon in conversations as it chokes out understanding. Don't use jargon until you understand what it means and what it implies. Having the "gist" of something is **"vibe understanding"** — that should be considered anti-engineering.

A simple solution: when faced with jargon, ask it to be explained. If it won't be — or you're ridiculed for asking — that is very revealing about the engineering culture you're part of.

> **Advice:** Just accepting the "vibe" of something without knowing the actual meaning is not a good characteristic for an engineer. Embrace jargon when you get the meaning; don't accept it blindly or use it to confuse.

## Foundational Thinking and Trade-offs

### Pace Layers and Technology Fashion

To grow expertise in software development, you need to see the essence of approaches and technologies rather than getting caught up in details. If you can't change your vision, you'll miss underlying sameness across technologies.

The **pace layers** concept (popularized by Stewart Brand) captures that technology is simultaneously fast and slow moving. The low levels move glacially while the high-level layers shift rapidly. Some call this the **fashion of technology** — and while fashion is very important in the short term for your career, chasing tech fashion long term is quite dangerous.

---

### Addressing Trade-offs

Many examples revolve around trying to settle on an appropriate approach. Notice the word "appropriate" as opposed to "correct" — there are many approaches and they vary greatly depending on project constraints.

You have to understand the essence of a problem and weigh the various requirements necessary to solve it appropriately.

> **SWE Tip: Solution-first thinking should be fought at all times.**

Solutions picked at any point always have trade-offs — you can't have everything. Gain performance, and dev time goes up. Make dev time go down with something simpler, and you may lose performance. Very often trade-offs are inherent in what we're doing — the nature of the solution has a baked-in compromise. In the age of AI-generated code, such compromises may be baked in without our awareness, often not surfacing until later (as you scale, when you maintain, when you have a security incident, etc.).

**Example — React:** React is a useful technology, especially if you're facing the problems it solves. Unfortunately, most people use it to solve problems it simply isn't well suited for. We even see people writing TUIs (Text User Interfaces) with it and then doing extra work to get performance to an acceptable place. Using a technology actually built for text interfaces would make the performance problem a non-issue.

> **Popularity ≠ engineering correctness. It just equals popularity.**

> **SWE Tip: Accept that trade-offs are inherent in ALL tools and tech.** The point is to find what values and characteristics your software must adhere to, and determine if the tool is appropriate to meet those goals. This is: (1) requirements and planning, and (2) achieving fluency and mastery of your tools.

## First Batch of Big Patterns

### Client-Server Model

The core model employed in web applications is **client-server**. A client (hardware + software) makes a request to a server (hardware + software) over a network.

**Three pieces:**
1. **Client** — software/system making a request (typically a web browser)
2. **Server** — software/system servicing a web request (typically a web server)
3. **Network** — the internet (public) or a private network (internal)

The client is where presentation and interaction happen; the server tends to be where data lives. Computation can be split:

- **Fat client** — client does the bulk of computing; thin server
- **Thin client** — server does the bulk of computing; client is just display

Your choice should be a function of the type of app and the -ilities you're trying to hit. Fat clients are likely not very securable in a public-facing situation; if security is an issue, that suggests a more server-centric approach. If security isn't the primary concern and you need massive scale, a client-centric approach might be appropriate.

**The client-side is:**
- Rarely under developer control (you can't tell users what device or browser to use)
- Possibly hostile (the user may not have positive intentions)
- Quite variable in technical ability (low-power phone to high-power desktop)

**The server-side is:**
- Under our control (we can dictate technology choices)
- Securable (though it must actually be secured; incoming requests can be hostile)
- Variable in technical ability in a predictable way (we can provision capacity as needed)

The major downside of the server-side is that it is **network constrained**. The network can be highly variable, and many developers suffer from making assumptions about the robustness and consistency of network communications.

**The Localhost Effect:** Working locally on your dev machine means you never see adverse network conditions. The localhost presents the web as a "happy path," and when sites are out in production the actual user experience may be very different. When building public-facing apps, be defensive in your engineering decisions in the face of a truly wild network.

### CRUD

**CRUD = Create, Read, Update, Delete**

This covers the actions we perform on a piece of data (resource). It is a simple idea, but it is at the heart of nearly every piece of business-oriented software.

CRUD apps are simultaneously:
- **Simple** from a technological point of view
- **Very hard** from a sociological and contextual point of view — what are we CRUDing exactly, and are there situational details that deeply matter?

This is roughly the concept of the **Domain** — what is the domain of our software? This leads to **Domain Driven Design (DDD)**: carefully mapping the domain of the problem into the code. Developers often lack command mastery of what the domain is all about and need to talk with stakeholders and domain experts who do, but who lack the information to write the code.

To ease translation from domain ideas to code, DDD suggests a **ubiquitous language** everyone uses — keeping code to product communication consistent. This echoes Spolsky's System Model and User Model idea.

> **Practically speaking:** the domain as the most important thing should animate your data model — table names, variable names, file names, etc. A developer with strong coding but little domain awareness is at risk from those who bother to understand a domain and have reasonable coding. If AI delivers code generation to non-coder types, being able to "speak" the language of the domain well enough and code fluently is the sweet spot.

### REST

**REST = REpresentational State Transfer** (Roy Fielding, PhD, ~2000)

Describes a method of defining resources as URLs and actions on those resources with HTTP methods:
- **URLs as nouns**
- **HTTP methods as verbs on those nouns**

Think of the URL as a "command line" — you consider the URL as a command prompt and perform actions on it with HTTP.

**Example:** `https://example.com/dogs/1` represents a "dog" entity with id `1`.

HTTP methods map to CRUD:
- `POST` — Create a new entity (C)
- `GET` — Fetch all entities or a particular one (R)
- `PUT` / `PATCH` — Fully update (replace) or partially update (U)
- `DELETE` — Delete a particular entity (D)

Note: we use "RESTful" quite often because we are REST more in spirit than precision.

**Be careful with endpoint design** — your API is the "user interface" that other developers will use. Consider expandability ("escape hatch"), avoid versioning issues, and document your API using the **OpenAPI format** (formerly called Swagger).

> **Tip:** Using mocks and API "contract style thinking" helps you work things out before committing to an execution.

### MVC

**MVC** separates application concerns into three distinct components:

- **Model** — Manages data, logic, and rules (DB / Data Store / State)
- **View** — Presents data to users (UI / State)
- **Controller** — Handles input, coordinates model and view (Code / State)

In web development, MVC often maps to:
- **Model** → Database/API interactions
- **View** → Templates (HTML/CSS/JS)
- **Controller** → Client or server-side request handlers (JS, PHP, or others)

As computation moves to the client-side, the pattern may adjust. MVC has many variations — MVP (Model View Presenter), MVVM (Model-View-ViewModel), and more — leading many to call it **MV\*** to show there is a theme but not a precision in this pattern.

The overall tension is between **fashion and foundation**. Fashion is the short-term approach and in many ways the safest market bet. Foundation is the long-term bet and what an engineer should always consider. Even if you end up going with the fashionable solution, better to document the decision in an ADR and be honest about the trade-off. That isn't being negative — it's being transparent.

## Closing Thoughts: Architectural Patterns — Promises and Problems

There is **zero doubt** we should leverage architectural patterns to solve problems. There is no reason to reinvent something when we can leverage the work that came before us. However, if we rush headlong into doing without understanding, we are doomed.

> **You should not apply a pattern until you deeply understand the problem AND the pattern you plan on using.**

Shift thinking leftward: start with high-level patterns and work toward low-level patterns. Do not look at code in an upward-only approach; move back and forth between problem and code as many times as needed.
