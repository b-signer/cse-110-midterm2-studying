# 13 — Operating and Evolving Software

---

## Premise

Once software is created and released into the wild, it will evolve and grow until its eventual retirement. Operating software is not something that tends to get much coverage in a university setting — it requires time and actual usage by end users, both of which are situationally problematic academically. However, once in industry you will likely encounter operational concerns that may be as hard or harder than any constructive effort you have experienced.

Traditionally, operating roles were handed off to specialized operations teams, but in the last decade the idea of **DevOps** has continued to expand — putting operations more in the purview of traditional dev teams. This aspect of DevOps focuses on observability, remediation, testing, and iteration. Collection of logs and analytics data is a key activity of this phase and will inform changes both immediate and longer term.

If operating software matures enough, the software becomes more of a system that grows and changes over time, hopefully better serving its users and owners alike.

---

## Key Support and Ideas

### Ops Isn't Separate Anymore — Introducing DevOps

The role of running software was often in the hands of system and network administrators rather than developers. The **admin-dev divide** left gaps and often resulted in frustration from both parties:

- Admins want stable systems with little change
- Devs often want/need change to evolve a system

The role of **DevOps** came in the early 2000s and has only grown since, as most software has taken a web-powered form. Devs are the ones who understand the software they built, so they need to have a heavier hand in running it.

> **DevOps ≠ CI/CD**
> - **DevOps** is cultural — it tries to join the creation and operation of software to break down organizational silos
> - **CI/CD** is more technical and focused on creating software; it generally stays within a silo and has a goal of building software reliably and quickly
>
> A downside of current DevOps culture circa mid-2020s: some managers use it as an excuse to make devs do more work with fewer people.

### The Engineering Insanity of Perfect — Availability Example

**Uptime/availability** is a key metric for measuring the reliability of networked software. Many organizations talk about achieving **"five nines"** (99.999%) or even perfect 100% uptime. However, 100% uptime is not a stable state — it is merely a passing moment. Given enough time, something will inevitably break: hardware fails, routers get misconfigured, network connections are severed, software bugs surface, and even cosmic rays can flip bits in memory.

**Availability percentages in practice:**
- 99% availability = 3.65 days of downtime per year (~7 hours per month)
- 99.9% availability = 8.76 hours of downtime per year (~43 minutes per month)
- 99.99% availability = 52.6 minutes of downtime per year (~4 minutes per month)
- 99.999% availability = 5.26 minutes of downtime per year (~25 seconds per month)

Even a seemingly small difference between 99% and 99.9% represents a massive engineering effort. Moving from 99.9% to 99.99% requires exponentially more investment in redundancy, monitoring, automated failover, and operational discipline.

Even well-resourced companies like Amazon, Google, and Microsoft have had major outages. The AWS us-east-1 outage example: ~2.5hr major impact, but the ripple effects lasted ~8hrs, affected 7,000+ SaaS and infrastructure players, with financial estimates north of $500 million USD.

**Pursuing high availability requires:**
- Redundancy at every layer (multiple data centers, redundant network paths, backup power, replicated databases)
- Sophisticated monitoring and alerting — detecting problems before users notice
- Automated recovery systems — humans are too slow for five-nines requirements in critical situations
- **Chaos engineering** — deliberately breaking things in production to test resilience
- On-call rotations and incident response — someone always ready to respond, 24/7/365

Each additional nine in availability roughly requires **10× the investment**. For most applications, the question isn't "how do we achieve perfect uptime?" but rather **"what level of availability actually serves our users' needs while being economically sustainable?"**

### The Need for Logging

Logs are one of those topics nobody likes to talk about, but we really need to deal with it. It's the inevitable thing you must have to actually run software.

**Log characteristics:**
- Logs need to contain as much data as necessary to find out what happened during some time period or incident — this often makes them hard to deal with due to the volume and cognitive load
- Log formats have two aspects:
  - **Data format** — standard (e.g., Common Log format) or custom definition
  - **Tech format** — TSV, CSV, JSON, etc.
- Logs can get unwieldy across two dimensions: file size and quantity
- Logs can be sensitive:
  - Intruders like to cover their tracks by deleting logs
  - PII can be found in logs, which if stored carelessly becomes a security/privacy problem

**Tactical Thoughts:**
- Plan what you want to log and at what granularity
- Plan where you will log (on server, off server local, cloud)
- Plan how much and how long you will keep logs (log rotation, offload, removal, rollup)
- Address how you will use the logs (search, dashboard, etc.)
- Treat logging as an important system and monitor it too

### Monitoring and Verification

In some sense monitoring is a form of verification like testing, but it happens more continuously. Given the time available and purpose, monitoring tends to be less complete than testing.

**Monitoring mapped to the -ilities:**
- **Availability** — check if app/site is reachable and functioning
- **Performance** — check if the site is performing at appropriate speed
- **Accessibility** — performance check plus issues like color contrast, keyboard navigation (should be static, but this may be a second check)
- **Compatibility** — browser checking and error capture; browsers and environments are moving targets; analytics also help
- **Functionality** — E2E-style monitoring; lightweight consistent execution of core tasks
- **Usability** — analytics focused on rage clicking, time on page, etc.
- **User acceptance** — analytics with outcome focus, feedback forms

Monitoring will also likely be risk-focused and project-specific. There is no general solution if you are doing this right.

---

#### Monitoring Practices

**Simple Access Checks (Synthetic) — ~unit level**

Be careful with surface-level checks — they can give false comfort.

> Q: Could an app be functional hosting-wise and completely broken?
> A: Absolutely! Surprise error pages in some cases return `200 OK`.

**Page-Specific Checking**

Instead of just looking for a `200 OK`:
- Fetch pages and look for certain strings or assets
- Fetch a special route `/diagnostic` which might have system checks on it (of course, this page needs to be monitored too)

**Open Questions:**
- How often do I access-check? 1min, 5min, 10min, 30min? The time can be related to the acceptable downtime. Faster checks are more intensive and costly, but less likely to miss downtime.
- Fast checks might also create "false positives" for small glitches.
- To address false positives and get a broader view, check from many geographic locations.
- Where you check from should be a function of who your users are.

**Scripted Scenario Checks (Synthetic) — ~E2E level**

A better check than availability is to perform tasks: click from page to page, fill out a form, perform a task. This is essentially E2E testing done as a monitor — it has the same challenges (fragility, time to execute).

> **Tip:** Aim to use real browsers when doing this — fake browsers aren't what actual users use.

**Real User Monitoring (RUM)**

Rather than a synthetic test, we want to check what is happening to actual users under their actual conditions.

- Start with technographic-style monitoring for reliability and performance
- Performance monitoring (use Navigation Timing API)
- JS Error Trapping (use `window.error` event and pipe back to your server)
- Capture user console output
- Capture full event streams to rebuild entire sessions (danger: starting to get into invasive territory)
- **Crash dumps** — traditional software relies on crash dump files; you can do the same with web software

**Monitor the Full Stack**

Monitor both front-end and back-end in a holistic way — something observed on the front-end may really be a back-end issue. Over-architected systems often have monitoring challenges surface when monitoring is set up.

### Analytics — User Acceptance and Software Improvement

The goal is **data-driven decision-making**. To do this, collect and analyze data from many operational aspects: basic measurable concepts like availability and performance, all the way to observing user behaviors and tracking beliefs about software value and satisfaction.

> Often when we attempt to see how our software is doing and aim to track **KPIs (Key Performance Indicators)**, they may seem slapped onto the software rather than designed into it. This reveals we didn't really do user-centered thinking properly and may suggest we need to use data to revisit that process rather than to cover up earlier mistakes.

**Types of analytics:**
- **Basic usage characteristics** — technographics and rough trends (e.g., Google Analytics, log files)
- **Advanced usage characteristics** — flow and in-page usage, replay analytics (e.g., Fullstory, heatmaps)
- **Simple feedback mechanisms** — thumbs-up/down signals on page
- **Survey-level and free-form feedback** — unsolicited or incentivized feedback

**Analytics-Driven Iteration:**

Data will often show things we could try or fix to make acceptance better. This requires formulating changes, implementing them, and tracking results.

- **A/B testing** — show some users one form of a page/feature, and other users a different form. Often implemented using feature flags/toggles.
- **Beta or pre-release mechanisms** — invite users to turn on something or join a beta to get feedback before wide release.
- **Dogfooding** — using your own product internally; a form of Alpha testing.

> **Tip: Mind the testing effect.** When users know they are under test they may act differently. Be careful not to "spook the animals" — we want data that is not overly influenced by awareness of being tested.

**The Danger of Analytics-Driven Design:**

Analytics do not always reveal actual intention, and chasing numeric simplifications can create huge problems.

- **The engagement fallacy:** Users clicking around = engaged users, the more users the more engaged the better. But: Unclear UI (or Dark Patterns) = users clicking around = "engaged" user who is also angry.
- **Silence ≠ satisfaction:** People often don't have time or interest to complain about software unless it's critical to them. Conversely, a user may be in a foul mood and one glitch sets them off — that single complaint may be the lone voice of an unreasonable person or may be the tip of the iceberg.

Engineering judgment is required to balance these signals. Organizational beliefs will often allow or disallow investigation of user concerns.

### Incident, Remediation, and Iteration

Once something is observed from monitoring, it is time to address it. Not everything can be at the same priority level.

**Prioritize monitoring by impact:**
- **Availability/Functionality break** = Big Emergency (app is down)
- **Performance degradation** = Mid-level Emergency (app slow, causing abandonment)
- **Usage issues** (accessibility, usability, acceptance) = Variable Emergency (users not doing the thing, partially unable to, or not doing it for unclear reasons)

**Responding to priority issues:**
- **On-call scheduling** — at least you know when you should expect to be involved
- **ChatOps** — chat is key for finding out about incidents and working with others to solve them; also for informing management of status
- **Status pages** — should NOT be on your own infrastructure
- **Social media updates** — having a presence to let people know about an incident is important; be careful the channel itself doesn't become a problem

In this sense, we see that we are just adjusting our software based on incident or analytical feedback — aka **iteration**. Software, when built correctly, is more like a **garden you tend** — things grow and change, you pull some things out and plant new things — rather than a **building you construct and then leave**.

---

## Conclusion

Operating and evolving software is fundamentally different from the initial act of building it. The software lifecycle of creation is more like software birth; this is the operation and growth of software over time until it is abandoned.

The shift toward DevOps has recognized an important truth: developers must remain involved in running the systems they create, breaking down the artificial divide that once separated development from operations. Yet this reality comes with hard lessons and split priorities — when trying to do all things, you might master few. Adding ops to the dev mix is a big lift.

Beyond the constraints of what humans can reasonably do, operating is generally just hard. Perfect availability is a passing moment, not a destination, and the costs of pursuing higher reliability increase exponentially with each additional "nine." The challenge isn't technical perfectionism but rather making informed engineering decisions about what level of reliability actually matters for your specific users and business context.

The operational phase should employ a systematic approach to observation and use this data for improvement. Using analytics and monitoring across the full application stack provides the visibility needed to understand how software behaves in the real world when it comes in contact with real users.

A data-driven perspective enables **continuous iteration** — treating software as a living system that grows and evolves rather than a static artifact left behind after launch (garden vs. building). Of course, it won't be easy. Like all aspects of SWE, it is a trade-off: maintaining system stability while enabling rapid changes, collecting rich operational data while respecting user privacy, and responding to genuine problems while avoiding decision-making paralysis from analytics that obscure rather than illuminate user intent.

Operating software well is as much about organizational discipline, clear priorities, and sound engineering judgment as it is about any particular technology or methodology.
