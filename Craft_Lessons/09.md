# 09 — Architecture

---

## Premise

Architecture is an important activity that is often missing or done on-the-fly in Agile environments. There is a tension about overdoing it as well, so like most aspects of engineering we need to find a balance.

We explore a number of common architectural patterns such as layered, microservice, monolith, MVC, and more. However, a pattern can be a crutch that makes things worse — at both the architectural and code level. We often fit the problem to the model as opposed to finding the model that fits the problem.

Since architecture influences everything and tends to include decisions that are hard to undo, we should take care and certainly document our choices. A common artifact to become familiar with is the **Architectural Decision Record (ADR)**, which serves as a reminder of the thinking that went into making a particular important decision. Diagrams are also key artifacts for architecture and should be kept up to date at appropriate levels of granularity.

---

## Key Support and Ideas

### Architecture is Very Important

As Martin Fowler says, architecture covers the important stuff and the hard-to-change stuff — if you believe that raw definition, you really need to get it right.

> "Definitionally, software architecture describes a piece of software's structure and its behavior, its design, its tradeoffs, and how those things might affect maintenance or scalability or whatever." — Greg Wilson

Architecture affects things both users and developers feel — the so-called **-ilities**, which include:

- Availability
- Maintainability
- Usability
- Security
- Performance
- Reliability

---

### The -ilities Should Drive Architecture

The various -ilities are the project characteristics we need to consider carefully when planning our architecture. Some, like scalability and reliability, are not directly user-focused. Many are:

- **Utility** — The system provides the required functions
- **Availability** — Ability to access the system and its functions
- **Performance** — Ability to access the system within acceptable time
- **Accessibility** — Ability to be able to use the functions
- **Usability** — Ability to use the functions successfully
- **Satisfaction (Satisfiability)** — Ability to enjoy the functions

These -ilities should be imbued into architectural decisions. Many of them are in tension, meaning we may not be able to achieve everything perfectly — we must accept trade-offs.

---

### Real and Fake Architecture Examples that Show "It Depends"

Two examples of non-code architectural success and failure: the **Sydney Opera House** (a failure that becomes a success) and the **Death Star** (a success that becomes a failure).

Both examples had huge architectural mistakes. One recovered, albeit with tremendous cost, to become a cultural icon. The other was massive and powerful and got taken down by a tiny spaceship. Both things exist in our tech world, so be very cautious of **survivorship bias** that allows you to wave away architectural impacts (e.g., a poorly built system that made people a lot of money — clearly that was the risky route!).

> **TL;DR** — Good architecture is more complicated than simple things like modularity and decomposition. It is often about fitness for use. Such external forces can wash away or magnify the effects of poor decisions.

---

### The Architect: Absent or All-Powerful?

Agile tends to focus on less globally defined roles, so architectural duties are not explicitly defined. In some ways, architecture is thought to emerge from the group with individual members making decisions — the **"Where's Waldo the Architect?"** problem.

The architectural guru / "all powerful" astronaut is at the opposite end of the spectrum. The challenge is that such a person is hard to find, likely becomes a single point of failure, and frankly becomes too distant to make good decisions.

The appropriate middle ground: we need an architect who is experienced, but who trusts their hands-on team members — or who is in the code with them.

---

### Tech Debt and Investment

Architecture tends to have long-term effects. We apply the ideas of **debt** and **investment** here:

- **Tech Debt** — Skipping steps, avoiding careful consideration, or rushing to implement incurs debt we will need to pay off later. Short-term decisions add up. The "interest" on tech debt grows as we need to fix bugs or as the velocity of making changes slows — the system becomes a **big ball of mud**.
- **Tech Investment** — Spending time now on research, education, codebase cleanup, or building proper foundations yields payoff later.

Tech debt isn't inherently bad — it may be acceptable to take some on for short-term gain, of course assuming you pay it off. Acknowledging the choice between debt and investment, rather than making it unconsciously, is the key. This requires judgement that only comes through experience.

### Balanced Architectural Decisions

#### Getting It Done vs. Getting It Right

In our market-driven world, so-called "scrap heap programming" or copy-paste coding does result in software that is **GETS** (Good Enough To Ship). However, just getting it done generally comes with a long-term cost — see debts and investment above.

#### Perfect Now Usually Not Perfect Later

Perfect tech decisions don't exist. Instead, aim to make the best decision you can at a particular time with a particular awareness of concerns. If those things change, the decision may no longer be good. **A perfect decision now might not be one later.**

Balancing tech decisions usually boils down to: solve the problem in front of you with awareness of potential future problems.

One example is what can be dubbed the **"Lambo Problem"** — worrying about which color Lambo to choose when you make it big, when that's clearly not a problem you're facing right now. In short: avoid making inappropriate decisions about scale, automation, and generalization too early.

We could summarize this as **premature optimization, premature abstraction, or premature automation**. Optimization might be useful later; abstraction might be appropriate when needed; automation essential as you scale — but probably not now.

### Common Architectural Catch Phrases

These catch phrases and concepts are good, but must be applied carefully — they tend to have counter-points.

- **Form Follows Function** — Let the system follow what it is defined to do. A key reason to think about the function of the software deeply before considering the form it takes, what tools to use, etc.

- **YAGNI** — You Ain't Gonna Need It. Keep the feature load down, the abstraction simple, and the tools minimal, because likely you won't end up needing them. Promotes minimalism and limits complexity for improved robustness and maintainability — and interestingly also tends to improve performance and security.

- **KISS** — Keep It Super Simple / Keep It Simple Stupid. Like YAGNI, but focused mostly on complexity.

- **DRY** — Don't Repeat Yourself. Like a single source of truth for code or an idea; duplication is bad since you need to find and update all occurrences. Generally fought with abstraction, but that can have downsides when taken to an extreme.

- **Embrace Trade-offs** — You usually can't have everything. Security might mean lower performance; higher security might lower usability, etc.

### Architectural Concept — Abstraction

Abstraction means hiding implementation details and showing only what is necessary to use something. It is part of "black box" thinking about the systems we depend on.

This relates to the idea of **private implementation details** — using an abstraction to guard against touching object internals. You may hear the idea of **data hiding**, which means keeping internal details of an object or module private so they can't be directly accessed or changed from outside.

- **Good data hiding** protects important data by exposing only safe, controlled ways to interact with it.
- **Poor data hiding** allows outside code to directly access or change internal values, risking bugs or misuse.

*Example:* A `BankAccount` class that hides the internal balance property and requires using `deposit()` and `withdraw()` methods to adjust it and `showBalance()` to view it.

Appropriate abstraction should allow you to more easily modify code without affecting unrelated pieces (modifiability, maintainability).

> **Note:** Abstraction mechanisms appear with increasing complexity: calling an API directly → wrapping the API → providing your own internal interfaces → using **Dependency Injection** to invert control (Inversion of Control / IoC). Even abstraction has trade-offs — always fight complexity demons wherever you find them.

---

### Architectural Concept — Decomposition

A key job is breaking code and systems into appropriately sized parts — a "Three Bears" problem: too big, too small, just right.

The **Single Responsibility Principle (SRP)** says each component should do one thing, leading to less complexity to reason about and test. Better decomposition increases granularity and makes reasoning easier, but introduces call overhead — a complexity cost.

When decomposition is applied across a network, each piece is called a **microservice** as compared to the larger combined style, which is called a **monolith**. Microservices provide single responsibility, but often introduce orchestration complexity and network failure risk.

Decomposition isn't something you do once — it requires revisiting over time. Be careful about over-decomposing early; that is premature abstraction. A **layer pattern** (things below providing services for things above) is a common decomposition — for example, **MVC** (Model View Controller).

---

### Architectural Concept — Cohesion

Related to decomposition is **cohesion**: how related and focused the things we are doing in a module, class, or function are. Are we keeping things together that belong together?

- **High cohesion** — everything in the component is related and working toward a single common purpose.
- **Low cohesion** — a "grab bag" where things are somewhat unrelated yet grouped together.

---

### Architectural Concept — Coupling

**Coupling** is the idea of how dependent things are on each other.

- If component A does not depend on B, they are **not coupled**.
- If A and B relate but are not deeply dependent, they have **loose coupling**.
- If a component depends on many others, it is **highly coupled**.

Generally we want loose coupling, as it is easier to swap things out and to reason about them independently. Coupling and decomposition are both balancing acts — sometimes over-decomposition leads to more coupling and communication overhead.

---

### Architectural Concept — Communication

Decomposed objects that are related need to communicate. The overhead and failure possibility of communication should not be ignored, especially in a networked environment.

Communication patterns may be **synchronous** or **asynchronous**, and communication events can be a good indicator of how a system is actually driven.

**Event-driven thinking** is quite important in networked systems and arguably models human-driven systems better than synchronous algorithmic thinking.

---

### Patterns of Architecture

The "technical" part of architecture for some SWEs is the idea of patterns. In physical architecture, Christopher Alexander's *A Pattern Language* introduced architects to a pattern lexicon for finding solutions. This idea was later adopted by the Gang of Four book and applied to software design thinking.

Key warnings:
- Patterns have a range of use — not every pattern fits every problem.
- Patterns have pros **and** cons; applying them blindly causes harm.
- Early pattern adoption without understanding the problem is a common failure mode.

---

### Capturing Architectural Choices — ADRs

If we make an architectural decision, we must admit that decision is quite important and potentially long-lasting or permanent.

An **Architectural Decision Record (ADR)** captures important decisions in easy-to-access and easy-to-consume documents for current and future developers. For this course the recommended format is Markdown / MADR.

---

### Architecture vs. the March of Time

Architecture should support change. Hopefully flexibility, modifiability, and maintenance have been made high priorities in the design — but good intentions aren't enough.

Even with good decisions, systems will need to change or they will fall to ruin. This shouldn't be surprising if you consider the software lifecycle or the simple idea of **"technical thermodynamics"** — a natural trend toward entropy.

Even catastrophic events can severely degrade or kill software. We should resist the assumption that change can always be addressed — and instead **embrace impermanence**. Let us not become Ozymandias the software architect.

---

## Conclusion

Architectural activities should be performed in a software project regardless of the process model adopted (Agile, Waterfall, hybrid, etc.). In short, these activities happen whether you are mindful of them or not. Making these decisions in haste or without thought can result in catastrophes — though we can get lucky. Do not let survivorship bias encourage you to think you can stumble upon architecture while you build.

Key phrases like KISS, YAGNI, and DRY are good ideas, but code-wise we should also focus on appropriate decomposition, coupling, and communication. Patterns in particular might help us get more practical about our choices and pick common idioms that other developers understand. However, even good architectural decisions may not persist. Over time, what seemed like a good decision may need to change — so architecture should be designed to evolve. Time wears systems down, so we need to plan for improvement and capture important decisions in diagrams and ADRs.
